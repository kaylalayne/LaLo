Who’s leaving a light on for you?

Ask. Listen. Stay.

Suicide is preventable. One honest conversation, one steady presence, one person who notices can change the direction of someone’s night. You don’t need to be an expert. You need to show up.

Why we leave a light on

Behind every number is a person who was loved, and people left behind who are still asking what they could have done. Silence and shame keep pain hidden. Talking about it openly is one of the most protective things a community can do.

Around 49,000people in the US died by suicide in 2022, according to the CDC.
Ages 10 to 34Suicide is among the leading causes of death for young people in the US.
Far more think about itMany more people have serious thoughts of suicide than ever attempt, and most who get support go on to live full lives.

Figures from the CDC and the National Institute of Mental Health. Check cdc.gov/suicide for the latest data.

Notice when a light goes out

Signs are not always dramatic. Look for changes from a person’s usual self, especially new, sudden, or growing ones.

What they say

  • Talking about wanting to die or having no reason to live
  • Feeling trapped or like a burden to others
  • Unbearable pain, emotional or physical
  • Saying goodbye in unusual ways

What they do

  • Pulling away from friends, class, or activities
  • Giving away things that matter to them
  • Big changes in sleep or eating
  • Increased alcohol or drug use
  • Searching for ways to end their life

How they seem

  • Hopelessness or deep despair
  • Rage, agitation, or reckless behavior
  • Sudden calm after a long low period
  • Extreme mood swings

Risk is higher after a loss, a breakup, a failure, bullying, a major life change, or when someone feels alone in a new place. If you see these signs, act, even if you’re not sure.

Myth or fact?

Tap a statement to find out. Misunderstandings about suicide, mental health, and asking for help are part of what keeps people silent.

“Asking someone about suicide plants the idea.”
Myth: Research shows asking directly does not increase risk. It often brings relief, because the person finally gets to say what they’ve been carrying.
“People who talk about suicide are just seeking attention.”
Myth: Treat every mention as real. Someone who reaches out, even indirectly, is asking for connection. Responding is never an overreaction.
“Suicidal crises are often temporary.”
Fact: With support, safety planning, and treatment, most people recover and find reasons to live. Getting through the worst moment matters.
“Only people with a mental illness think about suicide.”
Myth: Mental health conditions are a major factor, but suicidal thoughts can come from overwhelming stress, grief, isolation, or pain, in anyone.
“Suicide happens without any warning.”
Myth: Many people show warning signs in what they say, do, or how they seem, even if the signs are easy to miss. Learning them gives you a chance to act.
“Suicide is selfish.”
Myth: People in crisis are usually in unbearable pain that narrows their thinking until they can’t see other options. Many believe others would be better off, which is a symptom of the pain, not a lack of care.
“Going to therapy means you’re weak.”
Myth: Getting help takes courage and strength. Counseling is a skill-building tool, the same way a coach or trainer is, and millions of people use it, including many students and athletes.
“Strong people don’t struggle.”
Myth: Anyone can hit a breaking point, whatever their age, background, or grades, or how together they look. People who seem fine often hide pain the most. Strength includes admitting you need support.
“If I tell someone, I’ll just be a burden.”
Myth: Most people who care about you would rather know. Feeling like a burden is a common symptom of pain, not the truth. Reaching out gives others a chance to show up.
“You don’t have to be a professional to help.”
Fact: Friends, roommates, classmates, and family are often the first to notice. Asking, listening, and helping someone connect to care are things anyone can do.
“Once someone seems better, the danger is over.”
Myth: Risk can continue after a crisis eases, especially in the weeks after a hospital stay or an attempt. Keep checking in, even when things look calmer.
“Most people who survive an attempt do not go on to die by suicide.”
Fact: Support, treatment, and connection in the time that follows make a real difference. Follow-up care matters, because risk can stay higher for a while.
“It’s just a bad mood. It will pass on its own.”
Myth: A bad day passes. Depression, anxiety, or hopelessness that lasts for weeks or keeps getting worse is a health issue that responds to treatment. Don’t wait it out alone.
“Talking about suicide with care can save lives.”
Fact: Careful, hopeful conversation protects people. What hurts is silence and stigma. We say “died by suicide,” not “committed suicide,” because suicide is not a crime.

How to leave a light on for someone

These five steps come from research-backed guidance from the National Institute of Mental Health and the 988 Lifeline. Doing any one of them matters.

  1. AskSay it plainly and kindly: “Are you thinking about suicide?” Direct words show you can handle the answer.
  2. Keep them safeIf they have a plan or the means, help reduce access to anything dangerous, and don’t leave them alone. Call 988 or 911 if the danger is immediate.
  3. Be thereListen without judging or fixing. Let them talk. Your calm attention says “you matter.”
  4. Help them connectOffer to sit with them while they call or text 988, or to walk with them to a counselor, doctor, or campus counseling center.
  5. Follow upCheck in the next day, then the next week. Staying in touch lowers risk and shows the connection is real.

What to say, and what to avoid

Try
  • “I’m worried about you. Can we talk?”
  • “That sounds really painful. I’m glad you told me.”
  • “You’re not alone. I’m staying right here.”
Avoid
  • “You have so much to live for.”
  • “Think of how this would hurt others.”
  • Promising to keep it a secret. Safety comes first.

Words that help

The words we choose can make someone feel judged or held. Small swaps matter.

Instead of “committed suicide”say “died by suicide”

Suicide is not a crime, and “committed” suggests it is.

Instead of “a failed attempt”say “survived an attempt”

Surviving is not failing. This wording protects dignity.

Instead of “You’re being dramatic”say “I can see how much pain you’re in”

Name the pain. Don’t weigh it.

Instead of “Just think positive”say “I’m not going anywhere. Tell me more.”

Presence works better than a pep talk.

Instead of “Why would you do that?”say “What’s made things feel unbearable?”

“Why” questions can sound like blame. “What” invites a story.

Instead of “I know exactly how you feel”say “I can’t fully know, but I want to understand”

Honesty builds more trust than false certainty.

Ways to start the conversation

  • “I’ve noticed you seem down lately. How are you, really?”
  • “You don’t have to explain. I’m here.”
  • “Would it help to talk, or just to sit together?”
